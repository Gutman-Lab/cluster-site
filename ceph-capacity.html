Why Ceph capacity is set by the smallest host, not the biggest

Eight hosts contribute spinning disks, one 4+2 erasure-coded pool spans all of them, and the chassis are a mix of 8-bay R730s and 12+2-bay R730xds. The usable number is not the sum of the drives. It is four times what the least-free HDD host has left.

Snapshot, not a dashboard

Figures captured 21 November 2026 00:15 UTC from ceph df and ceph osd df tree on pmox2064. A bulk backfill was still running (about 11% of objects misplaced at roughly 0.85 GiB/s), so MAX AVAIL will drift as pmox2065 fills. Re-run the commands at the bottom before making a purchase.

The short version

Our bulk storage is one Ceph cluster built from whatever 3.5" drives we had. Eight hosts contribute spinning disks; six contribute only flash; triton is the small control-plane node. The three bulk pools are erasure-coded 4+2 with a host failure domain, and that single design choice explains almost everything about how much usable space we actually get:

Usable EC capacity ≈ 4 × (free space on the host with the least free space).

Not the total. Not the average. The worst host. Today that host is pmox2057 with 16.5 TiB free, which is why every EC pool reports 68 TiB MAX AVAIL. The other ~175 TiB of free space across the fleet does not add to that number.

This is not a bug. It is what “host failure domain” means, and it has real consequences for how we buy hardware.

What 4+2 actually does

All three bulk pools — cephfs_images_bulk (image pyramids), wsi-backup, and the RGW bucket data — use the same effective profile:

k=4  m=2  plugin=jerasure  technique=reed_sol_van
crush-failure-domain=host   crush-device-class=hdd

Every object is cut into 4 data chunks + 2 parity chunks = 6 chunks, and CRUSH places those six chunks on six different hosts. Consequences:

  • Raw cost is 1.5× (6/4). Usable efficiency is 2/3 of raw — before the host constraint.
  • A single host can vanish and every object still has 5 chunks (4 data + 1 parity), which is exactly min_size=5: readable and writable, with zero margin. A second host loss takes those pools read-only.
  • All three pools share one free-space pool. “68 TiB MAX AVAIL” is one number, not 3 × 68. Filling the image pool takes headroom away from backups and S3.

Why mixed drive counts dominate

Ceph does not divide total capacity by a redundancy factor and hand it to you. It places each PG’s six chunks on six hosts, weighing hosts by how much raw space they have. As data grows, every host fills at roughly the same percentage — not the same bytes.

That is the whole trap: a host with less raw capacity reaches 100% while a host with more still has terabytes free. Since every object needs a chunk on some host, the fullest host’s free space becomes the limit for the entire pool.

The clearest example is pmox2065. It joined recently with three 10 TB drives:

  • Today it is 8.3% full with 24.9 TiB free. It looks like headroom, and it is why the pool briefly reads 68 TiB.
  • Backfill will keep filling it until it is about 53% like everyone else. At that point it has ~12.7 TiB free — less than pmox2057 — and it becomes the new floor.
  • Result: the same pool that reads 68 TiB today would settle around 51 TiB with no data added.

Adding drives to a small host raises total capacity, but only helps the ceiling once that host stops being the smallest. And an empty new host always looks helpful for a few days before backfill catches up and reveals the real number.

Why mixed drive sizes matter too

The same logic applies inside the host. CRUSH weights OSDs by size, so a 12 TB drive carries roughly twice the PGs of a 6 TB drive, and fill percentages equalize. That is fine, with three practical caveats:

  1. Per-host totals are what matter for EC, not per-drive symmetry. A host with 7 × 6 TB (42 TB) and a host with 4 × 12 TB (48 TB) are close enough to behave similarly, even though their drive counts differ wildly.
  2. One small drive in an otherwise large host is not a big problem — it fills at the same percentage, so it just holds less. But a small drive in the only slot you have left can keep a host from rising off the floor.
  3. Rebuild and backfill are per-OSD. A 16 TB drive rebuilds far more slowly than a 6 TB drive, and backfill pressure lands hardest on whichever host is furthest below the cluster fill level. That is why a freshly added small host causes an outsized rebalance.

The R730 vs R730xd split is simply this in hardware form: 8 bays vs 12 (+2 rear). Our four R730s are effectively capped at 7–8 spinners; the R730xds can hold 11–12. If we fill all four R730s to 8 drives, they still total roughly 50 TiB each, and the R730xds will keep running ahead. That is not a problem to fix — it is a shape to plan around.

The arithmetic, with our numbers

The planning model is simple: total stored bytes stay fixed, every HDD host converges to the same fill percentage, and EC capacity is about 4 × the least-free host. Ceph’s own reported number is slightly more generous than that (it reports 68 TiB against a 16.5 TiB floor), so treat these as conservative planning figures, not promises.

Two things worth internalizing from that table:

  • Filling the small host first is worth more than buying a new chassis. pmox2065 has four spare spinners — three 6 TB and one 4 TB, none currently in Ceph — already sitting in its bays. Claiming them lifts the ceiling from 51 back to roughly 86 TiB. That is the single cheapest capacity decision available.
  • Every TiB of free space added to the floor host is worth about 4 TiB of usable EC capacity. Adding it to a host that is not the floor still helps a little, because it lowers the cluster-wide fill percentage, but the big multiplier only applies at the bottom.

After those two moves the floor passes to pmox2065 and pmox2059 at roughly 47 TiB raw each. Further gains need those hosts to grow together: each drive you add to one host raises the ceiling only until the next-smallest host takes over as the limit.

Practical rules we now follow

  1. Grow the floor, not the ceiling. Before buying anything, look at which host line in ceph osd df tree has the least AVAIL among hdd OSDs. That is the host to expand.
  2. Prefer filling empty bays over new chassis. Four spare spinners in an existing 12-bay box beat a new server with the same drives.
  3. Don’t judge capacity from a newly added host. Wait for backfill to reach the cluster’s fill percentage, then read MAX AVAIL.
  4. Never read MAX AVAIL per pool and add them up. The three EC pools share one pool of free space.
  5. Keep host totals within ~10–15% of each other when adding hardware. Beyond that, the smallest host silently caps everything.
  6. Match drive sizes when you can. Mixed sizes are supported, but a 4 TB drive next to 12 TB drives mainly exists to make the host smaller.
  7. 4+3 is a durability decision, not a capacity decision. Going from 4+2 to 4+3 tolerates two simultaneous host losses, but usable efficiency drops from 2/3 to 4/7 (~57%). It costs capacity; it does not create it.
  8. Watch the replicated pools. ceph_bulk — VM disks — is 3× replicated on these same spinners: 17 TiB logical costs ~51 TiB raw. That is about 12% of our hdd raw and 13% of the logical bytes stored on hdd, and it sits in the same failure domain as the EC pools.

Glossary for the non-storage reader

  • OSD — one Ceph daemon managing one disk.
  • PG (placement group) — the unit Ceph replicates or erasure-codes; objects are hashed into PGs.
  • CRUSH — the algorithm that decides which hosts/OSDs hold each PG. It is deterministic, weight-aware, and has no central index.
  • Failure domain — the level at which Ceph guarantees separation. Here it is host, so two chunks of the same object never share a server.
  • EC 4+2 — four data chunks, two parity chunks; tolerates one chunk loss (in our case, one full host) with no data loss.
  • min_size — the minimum chunks required for I/O. At 5 with 6 total, one host down is the edge of writability.
