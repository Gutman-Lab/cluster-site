Ceph capacity: the fullest OSD sets your headroom, not the biggest host

Eight hosts contribute spinning disks, one 4+2 erasure-coded pool spans all of them, and the chassis are a mix of 8-bay R730s and 12+2-bay R730xds. Usable capacity is the raw space divided by the code overhead — about 127 TiB of logical data. The ceph df number everyone quotes, 68 TiB, is something narrower: how much you can write before the single fullest disk fills up.

The short version

Our bulk storage is one Ceph cluster built from whatever 3.5" drives we had. Eight hosts contribute spinning disks; six contribute only flash; triton is the small control-plane node. The three bulk pools are erasure-coded 4+2 with a host failure domain, so the usable ceiling is simply raw HDD capacity ÷ 1.5:

407 TiB raw HDD → about 127 TiB of additional logical EC data before any disk is completely full.

The number ceph df prints as MAX AVAIL — 68 TiB today — is not that capacity. It is how much you can write before the single fullest OSD reaches 95%. The gap between 127 and 68 is imbalance: most disks have headroom, one disk does not. Imbalance — not mixed drive sizes, and not differing drive counts per host — is what costs capacity here.

This is not a bug, and it is the reason the same cluster can read “68 TiB free” while owning 191 TiB of unused raw space. It matters for how we buy hardware.

What 4+2 on a host failure domain does

All three bulk pools — cephfs_images_bulk (image pyramids), wsi-backup, and the RGW bucket data — use the same effective profile:

k=4  m=2  plugin=jerasure  technique=reed_sol_van
crush-failure-domain=host   crush-device-class=hdd

Every object is cut into 4 data chunks + 2 parity chunks = 6 chunks, and CRUSH places those six chunks on six different HDD hosts. Consequences:

  • Raw cost is 1.5× (6/4). Usable efficiency is 2/3 of raw: 407 TiB raw holds about 271 TiB of logical data in total, of which 216 TiB raw (≈144 TiB logical) is already used.
  • A single host can vanish and every object still has 5 chunks (4 data + 1 parity), which is exactly min_size=5: readable and writable, with zero margin. A second host loss takes those pools read-only.
  • All three pools share one device class. The identical “68 TiB MAX AVAIL” is one number, not 3 × 68. Filling the image pool takes headroom away from backups and S3.
  • Host count matters for failure tolerance, not capacity. With 8 HDD hosts, 4+2 survives two host losses only while 6 remain reachable.

The rule behind MAX AVAIL

ceph df answers a narrow question — how much can I write before the fullest disk trips the full ratio — and it does so exactly. The figure is computed per device class from the hottest single OSD, not from hosts and not from the fleet total:

MAX AVAIL = class_raw × (full_ratio − hottest_OSD_fill) × k ÷ (k + m)

full_ratio is 0.95 on this cluster and k=4, m=2. Today the hottest HDD OSD (osd.40, on pmox2057) sits at 69.97%, so 407.31 × (0.95 − 0.6997) × 4 ÷ 6 = 67.98 TiB — exactly what ceph df reports for every 4+2 pool. The same formula on the 3×-replicated pools is equally exact: 407.31 × 0.2503 ÷ 3 = 33.99 TiB for ceph_bulk, and 16.26 × (0.95 − 0.653) ÷ 3 = 1.61 TiB for ceph_nvme.

The practical reading: MAX AVAIL is a headroom-to-the-fullest-disk figure. It tells you how close the worst disk is to full, not how much storage the fleet owns. When one disk is at 70% and the rest are near 50%, the reported number is roughly half the real capacity.

Why mixed sizes and mixed drive counts don’t strand capacity

Ceph weights OSDs by size — crush_weight is the OSD’s capacity in TiB — so every disk attracts new data in proportion to what it holds, and fill percentage equalises by construction. A 6 TB drive and a 12 TB drive end up at the same percentage; the 6 TB simply holds half as much. A 7-drive host and an 11-drive host also converge to the same percentage; the 7-drive host holds less in total, but it is not “used up” early and it does not cap anyone else.

That is worth stating plainly because the intuition runs the other way: it feels like the small host should run out first. It does not, as long as its weights are honest.

What genuinely costs capacity:

  1. Weights that no longer match reality. Replaced a 6 TB with a 12 TB and left the old weight? That disk runs hot or cold relative to the fleet, and the hot one sets MAX AVAIL.
  2. A partial or interrupted rebalance. This is our state today: the newest host is at 10.2% while the oldest is at 62.1%. That 52-point spread is exactly why MAX AVAIL reads 68 instead of ~114.
  3. Thin provisioning. Allocated-but-unwritten volumes consume nothing until written, then arrive in a burst.
  4. Replicated pools on the same spindles. ceph_bulk (VM disks) is 3× replicated: 17 TiB logical costs 51 TiB raw — about 12% of all HDD raw for 13% of the logical bytes on hdd.

Diagnostic: ceph osd df prints a VAR column — each OSD’s utilisation relative to the fleet average. Above ~1.2 is a reweight candidate; that is the lever that moves MAX AVAIL, and it is free.

The R730 vs R730xd split is simply this in hardware form: 8 bays vs 12 (+2 rear). The four R730s are effectively capped at 7–8 spinners; the R730xds can hold 11–12. That is a shape to plan around, not a defect to fix.

80 TiB of EC data needs 120 TiB of raw space. Against 191 TiB free that fits with about 71 TiB of slack — the only thing that makes it marginal today is the hot disk:

Two things worth internalizing from that table:

  • Evening out the fill is worth more than buying a new chassis. The backfill now running lifts MAX AVAIL from 68 to roughly 114 TiB without a single drive added. That is the cheapest capacity decision available.
  • Claiming pmox2065’s four spare spinners — three 6 TB and one 4 TB, installed but not in Ceph (~20 TiB) — adds another ~12 TiB of headroom on top.

Practical rules we now follow

  1. Watch the fullest OSD, not the fullest host. Run ceph osd df and look at the top of the %USE column and the VAR column. Those are what set MAX AVAIL.
  2. Let rebalancing finish before you judge capacity. A newly added host or drive shows a flattering number until data lands on it.
  3. Prefer filling empty bays over new chassis. Four spare spinners in an existing 12-bay box beat a new server with the same drives.
  4. Never read MAX AVAIL per pool and add them up. The three EC pools share one device class and one free-space pool.
  5. Keep OSD weights honest. If a drive changed size, reweight it. A stale weight is the one way mixed sizes actually hurt.
  6. Match drive sizes when you can. Mixed sizes are supported and self-balance, but a 4 TB drive next to 12 TB drives mainly exists to make the host smaller.
  7. 4+3 is a durability decision, not a capacity decision. Going from 4+2 to 4+3 tolerates two simultaneous host losses, but usable efficiency drops from 2/3 to 4/7 (~57%). It costs capacity; it does not create it.
  8. Watch the replicated pools. ceph_bulk — VM disks — is 3× replicated on these same spinners: 17 TiB logical costs ~51 TiB raw, in the same failure domain as the EC pools.

  • MAX AVAIL — headroom before the fullest OSD hits 95%. Driven by one disk, not additive across pools, and it moves when data moves.
  • USED is raw consumption including parity/replicas; STORED is the logical amount.
  • The identical MAX AVAIL on the three EC pools is the tell: they share one device class and one free-space pool.
  • Absolute ceiling — for a 4+2 pool, free hdd raw ÷ 1.5 (191 ÷ 1.5 ≈ 127 TiB today).
  • Nearfull / backfillfull / full — 85% / 90% / 95% per OSD. The first warns, the second stops backfilling onto that disk, the third stalls writes.
  • ceph osd df tree is the view that matters for planning. Sort by %USE, ignore ssd/nvme/SSDoptane rows when reasoning about bulk pools.

Glossary for the non-storage reader

  • OSD — one Ceph daemon managing one disk.
  • PG (placement group) — the unit Ceph replicates or erasure-codes; objects are hashed into PGs.
  • CRUSH — the algorithm that decides which hosts/OSDs hold each PG. It is deterministic, weight-aware, and has no central index.
  • CRUSH weight — the share of data a disk attracts; set to its size in TiB.
  • Failure domain — the level at which Ceph guarantees separation. Here it is host, so two chunks of the same object never share a server.
  • k + m — data + parity chunks per object. 4+2 means 1.5× raw overhead.
  • min_size — the minimum chunks required for I/O. At 5 with 6 total, one host down is the edge of writability.
  • Misplaced objects — data sitting on the wrong disk during a rebalance.

Correction

An earlier revision of this page said usable capacity was “four times the free space on the least-free host” and that filling pmox2065 would drop headroom from 68 to ~51 TiB. Both were wrong. Adding capacity cannot reduce capacity; the falling figure was a ceph df estimate tracking the hottest OSD. Corrected 21 November 2026.
