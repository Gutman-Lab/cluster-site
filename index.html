What this site is

This is the public notebook for the compute and storage behind GutmanLab's pathology imaging work at Emory. It is part hardware tour, part operations log, and part benchmark archive. The cluster was not purchased as a single system; it accumulated. Each node has its own generation, RAM speed, drive mix, and personality.

Two clusters are documented here:

pmoxprime — the main cluster

15 nodes · Ceph Squid

Four Ceph device classes — HDD, SATA SSD, NVMe, and Optane-backed SSD — isolated by CRUSH rules. Pools are mapped to workloads instead of pretending every disk is interchangeable. This is where the Digital Slide Archive data, VMs, and research compute live.

Why "Frankencluster"

Most clusters are bought in one purchase order: identical nodes, identical drives, identical warranty dates. This one grew from hand-me-downs, surplus finds, grant leftovers, and temporary measures that became permanent. Three generations of server hardware share one Ceph cluster. RAM runs at 2133, 2400, and 2666 MT/s. Drive classes span 6 TB spinning disks to Intel Optane-accelerated SSDs.

The interesting engineering question is not "can heterogeneous hardware work?" It obviously can. The question is which workload should land on which tier, and how much performance a small configuration change can unlock. That is what the benchmark section is for.

The short version of what we learned

  • CRUSH device classes are the abstraction that makes this sane. One cluster, four tiers, explicit pool-to-tier mapping.
  • Reads hit the client link first. Every tier reads at about 0.93–1.02 GB/s from one 10 GbE client. Faster drives do not move that number.
  • BlueFS DB/WAL offload keeps HDDs close. HDDs with NVMe WAL reached 1,324 4 KB write IOPS and 529 MB/s on 4 MB writes — 71% and 57% of the SATA SSD tier, from spinning disks.
  • ZFS defaults can cost you an order of magnitude. Three settings took one TrueNAS pool from 375 to 4,271 MiB/s with zero hardware changes.
  • Verify the benchmark harness. A wrong grep string silently reported wrong IOPS for weeks.

The clean-run result: reads are the client, writes are the tiers

Every tier — HDD included — read 4 MB objects at 0.93–1.02 GB/s from one 10 GbE client, so the read table mostly measures the network. Writes separate the hardware: SATA SSD led at 934 MB/s, up from 431 in September after a PG increase and rebalance; HDD reached 529 MB/s with NVMe WAL offload. The earlier "HDDs beat SSDs on small writes" result did not survive the clean rerun, which is why we re-measured before publishing it as a rule.

About the numbers

Snapshot, not live telemetry

The September 2026 baseline was run against 14 nodes and 99 OSDs. The clean rerun on 22 November 2026 measured all four tiers on an idle cluster with 15 nodes and 109 OSDs (531 TiB raw at the 20 November capacity snapshot) and zero recovery. Results are published as measured, not extrapolated. Where a figure is current rather than historical, this site says so.
